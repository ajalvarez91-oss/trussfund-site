Two example schedules, tracked

Something landing on every pay day.

A weekly fund pays on the same weekday each week, and a monthly fund in the same week of each month. Hold one fund for each slot and a payment lands on every one of them. A new pair of examples is drawn up every Monday, written down, and followed from then on.

How a fund gets its slot. It has to qualify first: over the twelve months before the pick, its price must have held or risen while it paid. Each qualifier is then ranked against every other fund on the same schedule on three things, and the ranks are blended. Total return counts for half. Steadiness, the smallest fall from a high, counts for 30%. Breadth counts for 20%: an index or a mixed book scores full marks, a single sector half, a single stock or coin nothing. The highest blend in each pay slot takes it. A slot marked * had no qualifier and shows the fund that gave back the least.

Why the income sleeve moves slowly

There is an obvious way to run an income book: every month, sell whatever lagged and buy whatever is paying most now. It looks like diligence. It quietly costs you the better tax rate on every dividend you collect.

A dividend is only “qualified” — taxed at long-term capital gains rates instead of as ordinary income — if you held the shares more than 60 days inside the 121-day window around the ex-dividend date. That is 26 U.S.C. §1(h)(11), and it is a holding-period test, not a good-intentions test.

So a sleeve rotated weekly or monthly never clears the bar. Not “sometimes” — never, on any position, by construction. Every distribution lands as ordinary income. Rotating every two months is worse than it sounds, because it lands right on the boundary and fails unpredictably depending on where each ex-date happens to sit.

Which is why this sleeve is judged on the health of what it holds rather than on last month’s performance, and why it turns over slowly enough for the holding period to complete. The boring version keeps the better tax rate. That is not a forecast about returns — it is the rate the same dollar of income gets taxed at, which is one of the few things you can actually control.

Education, not tax advice. Holding-period mechanics have exceptions this summary does not cover, and your rate depends on your situation — talk to your own advisor.
The era of agentic capital

Reading is cheap now. Deciding is not.

For most of the last century the edge in managing money was labor. Someone had to read every filing, every distribution notice and every price, and that someone drew a salary. So the reading was done properly for people with a million dollars and summarized for everyone else.

That labor now costs close to nothing. The screen above is a year of prices and every payout for every fund on it, pulled and checked by software agents. Nobody was paid to read a notice. The same work, done by hand, is what a research desk used to be.

Judgment did not get cheaper. An agent that reads faster knows nothing more about next year. We have tested dozens of trading ideas this way and written most of them down as refuted. Speed made the testing affordable, and the ideas were still wrong.

So the rule here is short. Agents read, count and argue with each other. A person decides. A product that trades your money while you sleep has automated the one step that should stay with you.
